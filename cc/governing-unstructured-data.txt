#EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This white paper proposes a storage architecture to solve a lingering problem in data governance: controlling sensitive unstructured data. The paper argues that a multi-protocol file server integrated with cross-platform access control establishes a secure framework to consolidate, protect, and audit unstructured data, reducing the need to develop complex interdepartmental processes that can take years to put in place.

To measure effectiveness, the architecture includes a high-performance activity monitoring system to track unstructured content in a security-aware context of user identities, access patterns, and file events. An integrated application for managing sensitive folders and files empowers users to govern unstructured data. 

In addition, the storage technology integrates big data analytics with file server technology to heighten the strategic importance of IT: Tasks such as discovery, classification, and data governance become easier. Such services can increase competitive advantage and revenue while reducing costs and mitigating risks. 

But the real value of the architecture is that it enables you to get around several long-standing barriers to enterprise data governance: autonomous departments, lack of consensus, and complex processes. Using technology to bypass these barriers can ameliorate the need to change your organization's behavior and processes while empowering the data governance program to fulfill its promise of managing risk, guaranteeing data integrity, and creating value.

INTRODUCTION
Unstructured data is growing faster than all other types of data and will increase by 800 percent during the next five years. Analysts are referring to it as the big-data explosion.

But big data brings new challenges. Because 40 percent of it typically contains sensitive information, compliance regulations or internal policies mandate security. At the same time, workers depend on easy, rapid access to do their job. Security, then, must be balanced with availability. Easier said then done: Data silos, access barriers, different protocols, security gaps, and a lack of control stand firmly in the way, increasing risks and costs.

#OUTCOMES: WHY GOVERN UNSTRUCTURED DATA?
Organizations undertake initiatives to govern unstructured data for reasons that vary by organization, company or institution, industry, and objective. Some common reasons or targeted outcomes are as follows, many of which tend to be interrelated:

<!-- bulleted list in this section with first word and trailing period set in bold for emph -->

*<b>Compliance.</b> Develop a data control framework to help demonstrate compliance with regulations, legal requirements, industry standards, and internal security policies.

*Security and confidentiality. Protect stored secrets, intellectual property, private and confidential data, and other information assets. 

*Risk mitigation. Mitigate risk of security breeches, data loss or exposure, fraud, noncompliance, and costly legal problems. 

*Access and availability. Provide rapid access to intellectual property and business-enabling data to drive business objectives and to foster innovation, revenue, and the creation of value.

*Quality and integrity. Ensure that important data is trustworthy, accurate, and up to date to support transactions, decision making, research, and other business functions. 

*Cost reduction. All the elements in this list play a role in reducing costs in a variety of ways, from avoiding duplication of data or documents to keeping down the costs of storing and managing data. Decisions based on accurate information can radically reduce cost.

*Innovation, value creation, profit. All the outcomes in this list, of course, point to the bottomline of businesses: increasing revenue.

#INHIBITORS: BARRIERS TO GOVERNING UNSTRUCTURED DATA
Although the outcomes that a data governance program is intended to bring about are, at least at a high level, straightforward enough, achieving those outcomes in large organizations and complex enterprises is another thing altogether. Here are some common inhibitors to developing a data goverance program for unstructured data. Most of them fall into one or more of three groups: cross-enterprise complexity, complex organizations, and complex data. Most of the inhibitors translate into a requirement -- requirements that I'll return to later to identify the extent to which they can be solved by the right technical architecture for unstructured data. COMPLEX ORGANIZATIONS
*<b>Lack of awareness and sponsorship.</b> Administrators and users are frequently unaware of regulatory requirements for sensitive data as well as the importance of data quality to  decision makers. Without automated systems to force adherence and to monitor for lapses, users may inadvertently subvert  data goverance requirements. <b>Requirement:</b> Advocate for the importance data integrity and security; empower employees to take ownership of data goverance tasks with automated tools.

<b>*Lack of ownership.</b> A lack of awareness and sponsorship can result either in a lack of data stewards or in stewards who are not empowered to adequately manage data. "Best-in-class organizations are 2.2-times more likely than laggards to assign responsibility to <i>someone</i>," the Aberdeen Group says in an industry survey on securing unstructured data. The fallout from the mantra that data quality is everyone's responsibility is that the task goes unassigned. <b>Requirement:</b> Assign the task of governing important unstructured data to one or more managers and give them technology that makes it easy for them to govern data.

<b>*Lack of consensus.</b> Interdepartmental disagreements about how to process and store common data can undermine an enterprise-wide data goverance program. <b>Requirement:</b> Explore the prospect of using technology to skirt the need for interdepartmental consensus. 

<b>*Independent departments.</b> Departments empowered to act autonomously for business reasons can result in inconsistences in shared data as well as in data silos segmented by protocol: The engineering department stores unstructured data on NFS-based Unix file servers, while the research department uses CIFS-based Windows file servers. Data silos inherently hinder data governance because it is hard to apply universal controls across protocol boundaries. <b>Requirement:</b> Deploy multi-protocol file servers or NAS systems to eliminate data silos and foster universal controls.

<!-- SECTION SUBHEAD -->
COMPLEX DATA
<b>*Application data is increasingly going to file servers.</b> Application workloads are migrating to filers because it is easier to provision file-based storage than block-based storage. The application data further heightens the need for exception-based methods of monitoring and auditing. <b>Requirement:</b> Exception-based management of unstructured data and application data stored on file servers. 

<b>*Incompatible or conflicting security descriptors.</b> The security policies that are defined in security descriptors for files and directories are inconsistent across Windows and Unix systems. A Windows computer uses ACL; a Unix or Linux computer uses POSIX. Inconsistent security descriptors can limit access to files stored across different storage platforms. It can also result in file security being changed or removed, which can undermine the objective of mitigating risk and complying with regulations.  

<b>*Unrealistic scoping and different notions of what data is important.</b> When the scope of your data goverance program is too wide, the program can bog down in managing data that is not sensitive or not critical to your operations or objectives. Unrealistic scoping gets in the way of discovering and classifying important data. Meantime, different data owners might have different notions of what data is important, compounding the scoping problem. <b>Requirement:</b> A mechanism for marking data as important or sensitive and a method for tracking it in storage.

<b>*Data without metadata.</b> Unstructured data either contains no metadata or only limited, application-specific metadata, a void that makes it harder to discover sensitive information and classify it for protection.

<!-- SECTION SUBHEAD -->
CROSS-ENTERPRISE COMPLEXITY AND COMPLEX TECHNOLOGY
<b>*Silos of unstructured data.</b> Because of distributed environments, virtualization, disparate operating systems, and different protocols, unstructured data ends up in file servers segmented by protocol: Unix data goes to NFS-based storage devices, while Windows data goes to CIFS-based storage devices. For data governance, data silos are a major structural inhibitor with a pervasive, cascading effect: They hinder cost-saving storage consolidation, centralized monitoring, and universal governance. Data silos, in effect, can obstruct the development of a common framework to govern data. More importantly, data silos pose a barrier to the second major structural inhibitor: a common security model and uniform access control polices. <b>Requirement:</b> Consolidate data silos to a multi-protocol file server.

<b>*Inconsistent security policies.</b> A hetergenous network not only creates data silos, but also renders cross-platform access control difficult. In an enterprise, there are typically multiple identity management and access control systems, such as NIS or LDAP for Unix file servers and Microsoft Active Directory for Windows file servers. Multiple identity management and access control systems are a major barrier to ensuring quality, integrity, availability, confidentiality, and compliance while mitigating risk. <b>Requirement:</b> Implement a cross-platform identity management and access control system that can impose consistent security policies. 

<b>*Information lifecycle management.</b> In a mixed network, the problems associated with disparate access control systems and different storage protocols combine to sabatage information life cycle management (ILM) and records management. Cross-platform complexity inhibits the tasks of identifying, collecting, classifying, and controling sensitive information. 

<b>*Monitoring and auditing problems.</b>  Monitoring the effectiveness of your data governance program is another obstacle that stems from data silos. Compliance auditing falls victim to the same limitations: Monitoring and auditing technologies such as sniffers, agents, and shims can degrade the performance of your network and storage system. They also don't scale to manage the flow of data and metadata that is required for generating near-real-time contextual awareness of unstructured data. 

More importantly, however, monitoring technologies like SIEM tools are not closely tied to the identity management system -- blocking standardized audits that link users to identities and organizational roles.

<b>*Lack of comprehensive reports with visibility across silos.</b> Storage silos and mulitple access protocols impede enterprise-wide reports that aggregate data for compliance. Because there are different identity management systems for different silos, the reports cannot associate user accounts with their identities in the organizations, leaving you unable to demonstrate compliance with some regulations. 

<b>*Hetergenous storage systems undercut best practices.</b> There are commonly referenced best practices and guideliness to manage risks, which vary by industry but can include COBIT and ISO/IEC 38500.

The complexity, heterogeneity, and duplication of storage platforms and access control systems raises IT costs and impedes data goverance. There is, however, a technical architecture that can bridge these cross-platform problems so you can build a successful data governance program, bypassing the barriers associated with complex organizations and complex data.

The next section proposes an architecture. The subsequent section evaluates how the technology solves the problems and supports the use cases laid out at the beginning of the paper.

HOW THE ARCHITECTURE AND TECHNOLOGY ENABLE DATA GOVERANCE
This section explains how the architecture set forth above solves the problems and fulfills the technical and feature requirements of a data governance program for unstructured data. 

<!-- SECTION SUBHEAD:: -->
MULTI-PROTOCOL FILE SERVER ACCESSIBLE BY WINDOWS AND UNIX
At the foundation is a file server that is multi-protocol and cross-platform: It supports both the SMB/CIFS and the NFS protocols, making it usable simultaneously by Windows and Unix or Linux clients. A cross-platform, multi-protocol file server solves the interoperability problem that separates the data of Unix users from the data of Windows users, providing a consolidated approach to storage for users of all types of computers.  

Cross-platform incompatibilities have also been a hindrance to applying a uniform set of security policies. In the past, just as there have been different, incompatible storage systems for Unix and Windows users, there have also been different, incompatible identity management systems for Unix and Windows users. Unix clients have tended to use NIS or LDAP, while the de facto standard for Windows clients is Microsoft Active Directory. 

<!-- SECTION SUBHEAD:: -->
SECURE CROSS-PLATFORM ACCESS CONTROL
In this architectural schematic, therefore, the file server includes an integrated identity management service to authenticate users with Active Directory, NIS, or LDAP -- a component that, when combined with the multi-protocol file server, lays the architectural foundation for solving many of the problems in monitoring unstructured data.

The overall result is twofold. First, it frees your users from the bounds of platform-specific storage, empowering you to monitor all the stored data from a single system. Second, it secures the unstructured data by applying a common security model to it, enabling the monitoring system to associate data access with user identities and roles.

The integrated identity service also lets you control access to sensitive unstructured data and, as described below, monitor those controls for compliance.

<!-- SECTION SUBHEAD:: -->
COLLECT ACCESS DATA AND FILE EVENTS FOR ANALYTICS AND REPORTS
The event collectors and forwarders form the event monitoring subsystem. On the file server, the event collectors record data about viewing, moving, copying, modifying, or deleting directories or files. The collectors also capture changes to security descriptors. 

Over a secure connection, the event forwarders send the file events on to the NoSQL database, where they are stored in a highly flexible format. The NoSQL database allows the events to be manipulated for a variety of purposes, including big-data analytics, forecasting, and business intelligence. 

<!-- SECTION SUBHEAD:: -->
NOSQL DATABASE
The NoSQL database adds a unique high-performance layer: It digests events with write speeds faster than SQL databases and, more importantly, can easily scale horizontally to handle more events. 

Our commercially hardened SQL and NoSQL infrastructure with polyglot persistence is the only solution on the market today that can scale beyond a departmental deployment. Designed to support storage arrays with hundreds of millions of file objects and high workloads 

In this way, the NoSQL database becomes the basis for a powerful, flexible analytics engine that can correlate content types, sensitivity levels, modification attempts, security descriptors, user entitlements, access patterns, and patterns in content. The analytics system can, for instance, use data about past access patterns and file events to hypothesize about future patterns. These inferences can identify files that might contain sensitive material and need to be flagged for inspection. 

The NoSQL system, meanwhile, interfaces with a SQL Server database that segments frequently used data into columns and rows for reports and custom queries. The SQL Server also makes the data available to the dashboard for near real-time display of file events, especially exceptions, so that they can be acted on to deal with threats, breeches, and compliance violations. For extra security, the solution can easily be combined with interoperable storage encryption.

Enterprise Data Security The next most-mature approach takes an enterprisewide view of all data -- structured and unstructured -- that exists in the core of the typical enterprise and addresses the protection of that data throughout its life, including identification, risk assessment, access controls and controls enforcement across all data storage platforms. 

This approach is best characterized by offerings from Imperva and Netezza. Imperva treats DAM as a component of a data protection and risk management function. Netezza's Mantra offering provides support for structured and unstructured data and bundles DAM with an analytics engine and data-warehousing component as part of an enterprise data security program. Under this scenario, structured and unstructured data are located, categorized and classified, and access is monitored, not only in databases, but in the data center platform in the data center. This requires working closely with the business, performing classification and evaluation -- some of this can be done automatically -- and then building a complete data protection framework. One or more vendors may be adding capabilities for activity monitoring of unstructured data, to enable enterprises to understand what is happening with their Windows or Unix file shares, for example. Gartner believes this is an important potential development, and one that enterprises considering DAM solutions should follow closely, because a narrow focus on structured data is a long-standing weakness of DAM technology. Enterprises should also consider alternatives, such as security information and event management (SIEM) or some combination of SIEM, data loss prevention (DLP) and DAM offerings to support data security initiatives.

"All storage compliance involves hardware and software elements, but there is no single approach or architecture to rely on. For example, disk-to-disk, disk-to-disk-to-tape and disk-to-disk-to-optical storage platforms all have potential applications in compliance, but regulations rarely, if ever, define storage implementations. Organizations are left to interpret the rules, formulate the requirements and establish the technologies to meet legal obligations. THIS IS THE BIG FLAW IN STORAGE COMPLIANCE -- organizations jump to a "vendor's solution" that promises compliance, without fully understanding the laws and their impact. For example, a disk storage platform may offer reliability but might not provide the security or immutability demanded for your industry. "Buying an array for long-term retention is nice," says Jim Damoulakis, chief technology officer (CTO) at GlassHouse Technologies Inc. in Framingham, Mass. "But it doesn't make you compliant." Policies and processes must be implemented to manage storage compliance."--from https://searchstorage.techtarget.com/feature/Data-storage-compliances-impact-on-storage-product-choices (emphasis is mine)

"Workload is added in the monitoring, reporting and auditing capabilities needed to support storage compliance. "The thing that adds workload is that you now have to prove that you're doing it," Damoulakis says. In some cases, the cost can be substantial. Back in 2004, General Electric Co. revealed it spent about $30 million in compliance costs just to meet SOX regulations."

Data governance1 refers to the overall management of the availability, usability, integrity, and security of the data employed in an enterprise. A sound data governance program includes a governing body or council, a defined set of procedures, and a plan to execute those procedures. In practical terms, that means putting personnel, policies, procedures, and organizational structures in place to make data accurate, consistent, secure, and available to accomplish the business objectives. It can take on special importance because of the legal requirements and compliance regulations that it must fulfill in the face of rapidly expanding volumes of unstructured data. 

Unstructured data, industry analysts say, is growing faster than all other types of data and will increase by as much as 800 percent during the next five years. Respondents to an industry survey by the Aberdeen Group estimated that 40 percent of their sensitive data is in unstructured formats such as PDF and Microsoft Word and Excel.

"Rising volumes of content and an expanding array of electronically stored information combined with increasing litigation, staff shortages, limited integration with key applications, and other factors lead to low confidence and considerable expense during the eDiscovery process. Only 28% of message archiving stakeholders report that they are very confident that if challenged their organization could demonstrate that their electronically stored information (ESI) is accurate, accessible, and trustworthy" March 17, 2010
Regulatory And eDiscovery Demands Drive
A Growing Message Archiving Market
Q1 2010 Global Message Archiving Online Survey
